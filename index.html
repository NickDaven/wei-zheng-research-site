Research profile

I currently conduct funded research in two connected areas: using AI to develop students’ higher-order skills, and using predictive AI to understand future transportation infrastructure deterioration under uncertainty.

Two NSF projects connect students’ responses to open-ended questions with AI-powered assessment and feedback. A current MarTREC-supported project applies transformer-based sequence learning to forecast bridge corrosion-initiation probability across future time horizons.

My recent journal study combines a stochastic reliability simulator, historical probability sequences, scenario variables, and a Temporal Fusion Transformer. Monte Carlo dropout provides an approximate estimate of model uncertainty. These methods build on my earlier Bayesian inference and infrastructure reliability research and inform my continuing interest in risk-informed inspection, maintenance, and human decision-making.

Across engineering and education, I study how people evaluate evidence, recognize uncertainty, assess AI outputs, and develop critical thinking, metacognition, self-regulation, and collaborative problem-solving skills.

3. Transportation infrastructure forecasting and uncertainty quantification

Principal Investigator · MarTREC Project 7011 · Maritime Transportation Research and Education Center

This current funded research investigates AI-based prediction of reinforced concrete bridge deterioration. Current transportation infrastructure research

From sequence data
to forecasts and uncertainty.

In our recent journal paper, Yuzhong Huang and I formulate corrosion-initiation probability prediction as a multi-horizon sequence-learning problem: use a history of deterioration-related information to estimate how risk evolves over future time steps.

1. Organize sequence data

A diffusion-based stochastic reliability model generates controlled corrosion-initiation probability trajectories. Historical sequences, scenario variables, and future or scenario-defined inputs form the forecasting task. This simulation testbed prepares a structure for future inspection, sensor, laboratory, and exposure histories.

2. Predict future trajectories

A Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) learns the relationship between sequence history, covariates, and future probability trajectories. Its attention and variable-selection structure supports interpretable multi-horizon forecasting. The study compares TFT with recurrent and other predictive baselines.

3. Quantify uncertainty

Stochastic reliability modeling represents uncertainty in the simulated deterioration process. Repeated predictions with MC Dropout estimate approximate model-level epistemic uncertainty, reporting predictive means, dispersion, and approximate 95% predictive intervals.

Connecting prediction to decisions

The aim is to help engineers interpret both forecast risk and confidence in the prediction when comparing inspection needs, maintenance timing, and infrastructure management options. This continues my research on Bayesian inference, reliability, and decision-making under uncertainty.

The published study is a simulation-based proof of concept. Its MC Dropout intervals describe approximate model uncertainty within that setting, rather than a complete or field-calibrated account of all deterioration uncertainty. Field data validation remains necessary before operational use.

Research interests · AI evaluation and engineering ethics

Evaluating AI.
Making ethical decisions.

My research explores how large language models (LLMs) and knowledge graphs can support human decision-making under uncertainty, engineering ethics, and the ability to evaluate AI-generated advice. The aim is to strengthen human reasoning and keep people responsible for their final decisions.

Principles and rules

A knowledge graph connects relevant evidence, principles, rules, stakeholders, risks, and conflicts. Graph-based retrieval gives the LLM structured context for guiding deliberation.

Reflection and questions

A second graph records what the person has considered. Comparing the two helps identify overlooked issues and guide targeted LLM questions. The human makes and justifies the final decision.

Skills for evaluating AI

Check evidence, recognize uncertainty, examine alternatives, consider ethical and community impacts, and decide when to accept, challenge, revise, or decline AI advice.

The proposed international, community-based research would examine how cultural, disciplinary, and stakeholder perspectives shape ethical judgment, including comparative U.S., UK, and Australian perspectives. Engineering scenarios would ask who benefits, who bears risk, whose voice is missing, and how a decision affects different communities. Differences would be examined within groups as well as across them.

The same structure could support student learning through guided questioning and feedback. Students would practice checking AI claims against evidence and relevant principles, recognizing uncertainty and limitations, examining alternatives and community impacts, and deciding when to accept, challenge, revise, or decline AI advice. Proposed evaluation would examine reasoning quality, detection of unsupported or conflicting advice, appropriate reliance, and transfer to new problems, comparing different forms of AI support with human-only deliberation.

Knowledge graphs support traceability and evaluation; their use does not guarantee correct or ethically acceptable AI outputs.
